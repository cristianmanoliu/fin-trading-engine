# Why are some symbols profitable and others not?

Analysis run 2026-05-05 against the continuous 5y backtest + OOS persistence test data. Sources: `results/symbol_characteristics.csv`, `results/option_c_57sym_continuous_2026-05-05.txt`, `results/oos_persistence_2026-05-05.tsv`. Generated by `scripts/why_some_symbols_work.py`.

## TL;DR — the surprising answer

**Most of the per-symbol PnL difference is noise. The structural differences between the top-10 and bottom-10 are tiny.** There is *some* real per-symbol alpha (statistically detectable: χ²=94.2, p<0.001), but the magnitude is only **1.5× the size of pure chance** for the trade counts we have. The simple price-action metrics (vol, drawdown, trend, Hurst exponent) **do not meaningfully predict** which symbols win.

This is the same conclusion as the OOS persistence test (Spearman ρ=+0.25, train top-16 lost 69% of edge OOS), reached from a completely different angle. **Two independent lines of evidence agree: per-symbol selection is mostly luck.** The aggregate strategy edge is real and consistent (~+$3M per half across 57 symbols), but you cannot reliably predict which 16 of 57 will be the winners.

## The structural similarity is the headline finding

Top 10 vs bottom 10 by 5y continuous PnL — averages of their structural metrics:

| Metric | Top 10 (winners) | Bottom 10 (losers) | Difference |
|--------|-----------------:|------------------:|-----------:|
| Annualized volatility | **1.15** | **1.17** | virtually identical |
| Max drawdown | **−89%** | **−92%** | nearly identical (all cryptos crash hard) |
| Hurst exponent | **0.571** | **0.585** | nearly identical (slightly trending) |
| Direction-persistence (1d) | **0.473** | **0.471** | identical |
| Avg daily range / close | **8.6%** | **9.1%** | similar |
| 1-day return autocorrelation | −0.06 | −0.02 | small but real (see below) |
| 5y total return | **+10.5×** | **+1.4×** | only meaningful gap |

**Read this carefully.** Apart from "winners had bigger 5y total returns" (and that's circular — assets that went up are easier to ride up), there is no structural fingerprint that distinguishes a strategy-winner from a strategy-loser. RUNEUSDT (top, +$833k) and CRVUSDT (bottom, −$796k) have nearly the same volatility (1.42 vs 1.42), same max drawdown (−96% vs −97%), and similar Hurst exponent (0.58 vs 0.60).

If the structural-alpha hypothesis were correct, the top winners would *look different* from the bottom losers in measurable ways. They don't.

## Statistical significance test

H₀: every symbol has the same true win rate (the pooled 16.95%); observed differences are binomial noise.

| Quantity | Value | Interpretation |
|----------|-------|----------------|
| Pooled WR | 16.95% | barely above the 16.67% breakeven for 5R |
| χ² statistic (56 df) | 94.2 | reject H₀ at p < 0.001 |
| Observed WR std-dev across symbols | 0.66pp | |
| Expected WR std-dev under pure noise | 0.45pp | |
| **Ratio observed / chance** | **1.46×** | |

H₀ is rejected, so per-symbol differences are not *pure* noise — but the ratio of 1.46× tells the real story: **about 32% of the per-symbol spread is structural, the other 68% is chance.** A symbol that beat the pooled WR by 1pp in our 5y window will, on a fresh 5y window, regress most of the way back toward the pooled mean.

This is exactly consistent with the OOS persistence test result.

## What about the correlations with structural metrics?

Spearman ρ between each metric and continuous PnL across all 57 symbols:

| Metric | ρ vs PnL | ρ vs Win-Rate | ρ vs OOS test PnL |
|--------|---------:|--------------:|------------------:|
| 1-day return autocorrelation | **−0.403** ⭐ | −0.340 | −0.321 |
| Hurst exponent | −0.293 | −0.326 | −0.330 |
| 5-day return autocorrelation | −0.253 | −0.153 | −0.172 |
| 5y total return | +0.173 | +0.086 | −0.023 |
| 20-day return autocorrelation | −0.160 | −0.146 | −0.002 |
| Annualized volatility | −0.107 | −0.095 | −0.160 |
| Avg daily range / close | −0.075 | −0.009 | −0.116 |
| Max drawdown | +0.038 | +0.057 | +0.111 |
| Direction-persistence (1d) | +0.113 | +0.209 | +0.086 |

**Only one metric crosses the |ρ|>0.4 line: 1-day autocorrelation.** And the sign is *counterintuitive* — more mean-reverting symbols (more negative daily autocorrelation) tended to do *better*, not worse. Hurst tells the same story (lower H = more anti-persistent = better strategy result, again counterintuitive).

The likely explanation: the strategy operates at 5m timescales with a 4H bias filter, so daily-level autocorrelation isn't directly the variable that matters. The negative correlation we see is probably a **proxy effect** — assets with daily mean reversion may have certain microstructure (frequent EMA crosses, varied entry contexts) that the strategy happens to capture. But it's a weak effect and not a clean causal story.

**Volatility, drawdown, and total return are essentially uncorrelated with strategy success.** The strategy does not require an asset to trend up, to be highly volatile, or to avoid crashes. It just requires *something* about the price microstructure — something we cannot identify from these macro metrics.

## What I think is actually going on

The strategy edge is in **microstructure** — something at the 5m-30m timescale that our macro metrics (computed at daily resolution) cannot detect. Possibilities:

1. **EMA-cross signal quality.** Some symbols have cleaner EMA9×EMA21 crossovers (price decisively breaks through one EMA, follows through). Others have noisy EMAs that produce many false crosses. We don't measure this.

2. **4H bias accuracy.** The 4H bias filter (long when 4H trend is up, short when down) has different predictive power for 5m moves on different symbols. Symbols where the 4H trend is a poor predictor of next-5m direction will lose.

3. **Sub-5m volatility character.** A symbol whose 5m candles tend to have small bodies and large wicks (chop) will see more whipsaw stops than a symbol with directional 5m candles.

4. **Slippage/fee-equivalent effects in the synthetic tick ordering.** Even with `--exact-fills` and `--include-boundary`, the synthetic open→high→low→close ordering may interact differently with each symbol's actual price path.

5. **Pure path dependence.** Different windows of 5y will produce different rankings. Our 2020-2025 happened to put RUNE on top and CRV at the bottom; a different period might shuffle them substantially.

To test these, we'd need to compute 5m-level metrics, which requires processing the full 1-minute data at sub-daily resolution. Not impossible but expensive in compute. **Whether it's worth doing depends on whether the answer would change anything you'd actually do** — and given the OOS test already concluded "selection is mostly luck, diversify," the answer probably wouldn't change the deployment.

## Practical implications for the live deploy

These findings reinforce what's already in `NEXT_STEPS.md`:

1. **Don't try to pick winners.** Two independent tests (OOS persistence + structural correlation) say per-symbol selection is mostly noise.

2. **The 11-symbol deployed set is fine.** It includes 4 of the structurally-strongest persistent winners (RUNE, SOL, LINK, APE) plus 4 mid-cluster names (BTC, ENJ, ETH, LDO) plus 3 less-validated names (XLM, TIA, ZIL). Diversification beats concentration.

3. **The aggregate edge is real and consistent.** χ² test rejected "all symbols are the same" at p<0.001, and OOS test showed +$3.16M in test period. The strategy works at the portfolio level.

4. **Forward expectation should be conservative.** The OOS test is the cleanest forward-looking number we have: ~$1.5M-$2M over the next 28 months across the deployed 11 at $1k stake. The +$3.64M continuous-5y figure is partly historical luck and won't replicate.

5. **Don't waste cycles trying to predict which symbols will win.** That's the trap the OOS persistence test caught, and structural metrics confirm: there's no signal you can act on. Just deploy a diversified set and let the aggregate edge play out.

## What I would NOT spend more time on

- Computing 5m-level microstructure metrics. Even if we found a strong predictor, it would be in-sample fitted; we'd need another OOS test to verify, and then we'd be back to where we started.
- Per-symbol parameter tuning. Same reason: in-sample fits don't survive OOS.
- More elaborate symbol-selection schemes. The OOS evidence says they don't pay.

## What might actually be worth doing later

- **Live forward validation (free).** After 30-90 days of live data, compute live per-symbol WR. Compare to backtest WR. If a symbol's live WR drifts >2pp from its backtest WR, that's a real signal — possibly a microstructure regime change or backtest/live data path bug.
- **A regime detector (moderate effort).** If you can detect "we're in a chop period" vs "we're in a trend period" (e.g., from cross-asset realized vol or a market-wide trend indicator), pausing engines during chop could materially improve risk-adjusted returns. This addresses the "trend follower in a range" failure mode.
- **Position-size scaling by recent symbol performance (low effort, high risk).** Reduce stake on a symbol whose last 30 trades underperformed; restore when it recovers. But this re-introduces the selection-bias problem we've been trying to avoid. Be very careful.
